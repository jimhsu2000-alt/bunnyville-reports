烏龍茶酒桶威士忌是什麼？先用茶酒潤桶，再讓威士忌過桶，整理官方說明給想入門的人
內文 Body
最近看到很多人在問：「烏龍茶酒桶威士忌」到底是在威士忌裡面加茶嗎？還是桶子本身有茶味？剛好龐尼維爾官網對這支酒的做法寫得蠻清楚，這篇就用比較口語的方式整理一下，順便聊聊喝法。內容都是照官網說明，不是我自己的心得，有錯再請大家指正。 【先講結論：不是在威士忌裡泡茶葉】 這款酒的重點在「桶子」。官網的說明是：先用台灣的優質烏龍茶，做成一款烏龍茶酒（茶的 liqueur），把這款茶酒放進精選的橡木桶裡熟成。等到桶子吸收了茶酒的味道之後，把已經熟成的單一麥芽威士忌換進這些桶子裡做第二段熟成，也就是「過桶」（finish）。 所以整個流程可以拆成兩步： 1. 茶酒入桶：烏龍茶酒在橡木桶裡熟成，官網說這個過程會讓桶子帶上單寧、蜂蜜般的甜味，還有淡淡的煙燻感。 2. 威士忌過桶：熟成好的單一麥芽威士忌再進到這些「吸飽茶酒」的桶子，慢慢吸收茶的細緻風味。 官網也提到，這個做法是參考傳統雪莉桶的概念：用別種酒先潤桶，再讓威士忌過桶，讓風味多一層。 【官方描述的風味】 官網的品飲描述大概是這樣： ・外觀：金琥珀色。 ・香氣：熟果、太妃糖、烘烤堅果、淡淡橡木，混著花香茶香，還有一點烘烤茶葉的氣味。 ・口感：質地滑順，有麥芽的甜，也有烏龍茶的花香與木質感，帶一點點煙燻。 ・尾韻：茶味的餘韻，加上橡木帶來的溫暖感。 標示的關鍵字是「熟果、太妃糖、烘烤堅果、橡木與花香」，以及「橡木、麥芽與茶」。看起來走的是「麥芽甜感為底，茶香當配角」的路線，而不是整杯都是茶味。 【兩個版本有什麼不同】 官網目前有兩個烏龍茶酒桶版本： ・｜烏龍茶酒桶 40%｜烏龍茶酒桶 原桶強度 ・容量｜700 ml｜670 ml ・酒精濃度｜40%｜52.3% ・基酒｜單一麥芽｜單一麥芽 ・桶型｜烏龍茶酒桶過桶｜烏龍茶酒桶過桶 [圖片 2] 兩款的描述同樣是「熟果、太妃糖、烘烤堅果、橡木與花香」，差別主要在濃度：原桶強度沒有加水稀釋，酒精感和風味濃度自然比較高。如果是第一次接觸，通常比較建議先從 40% 的開始；已經習慣烈一點的酒，再去比較原桶強度版。 【想試試看，可以怎麼喝】 下面是比較通用的喝法建議，不是官方指定： ・純飲或加一小塊冰：先聞聞看有沒有官網說的花香與烘烤茶葉味，再喝喝看麥芽甜感和茶香怎麼搭。 ・原桶強度版加幾滴水：加一點點水常常能讓香氣更容易打開，可以一滴一滴慢慢加，找到自己舒服的比例。 ・搭點心：既然是帶茶香和堅果感的酒，搭堅果、不太甜的點心，或烘焙過的茶點，風味方向比較接近，不容易互相蓋過去。 【跟其他茶酒桶系列的關係】 龐尼維爾的「酒桶系列」不只烏龍茶。官網的酒款列表裡還有茉莉綠茶、草莓、桑葚、青梅、玫瑰、荔枝、蜂蜜、金棗、檸檬、橘子、黑后與金香葡萄等酒桶系列，概念都是先用台灣的水果、茶或花入酒，再用那個桶子給威士忌過桶。有興趣的人可以把不同酒桶的描述放在一起看，會比較容易理解「同一款基酒，換一種桶會差在哪裡」。 【順手補充：這家酒廠的基本資料】 ・酒廠位在桃園龜山的「兔子坑」，2004 年 7 月 15 日取得製酒執照，算是台灣第一代民營酒廠（2002–2004 年核照）。 ・官網公布的蒸餾設備：壺式蒸餾器 6 座（3,000／2,000／2,000／1,000／1,000／200L），加上 2 座柱式蒸餾塔（7 公尺 20 層泡罩、9 公尺 32 層泡罩）。 ・官方寫的蒸餾產能是一年 100 萬瓶。 ・實體門市在台北大安區永康街 13 巷 1 號，想實際看酒款可以先查官網的營業資訊。 【小結】 烏龍茶酒桶威士忌的特色，是「用茶酒潤過的桶子」來做第二段熟成，而不是直接加茶。如果你本來就喜歡有點花香、堅果和太妃糖氣息的威士忌，這類酒款滿值得拿來比較看看。大家如果有喝過其他家的茶桶或類似做法的酒，也歡迎留言分享，這邊就當作交流。 （以上品飲描述皆為酒廠官網的官方說明，並非獨立評論。）

Pot still vs column still, and what a 7 m / 20-plate and a 9 m / 32-plate column are for: using one Taiwanese distillery's published specs
內文 Body
I saw a few threads recently asking why distilleries list things like "7 metres, 20 plates" on their spec sheets, and what that actually tells you about the spirit. Most distilleries don't publish much, so I used one that does — Bunnyville Distillery in Taoyuan, Taiwan — as a worked example. Everything about the distillery below comes from its own website; the general distilling explanation is the standard textbook version, and I'll mark where the two meet. ## Pot stills vs column stills in one paragraph A **pot still** is a batch machine: you fill it, heat it, run it, and empty it. Because it makes a relatively low-strength separation in each run, it keeps a lot of the character of what went in, which is why pot stills are the classic tool for malt whisky. A **column still** (also called a continuous or Coffey still) is a tall column with plates stacked inside. Vapour rises and condenses on the plates again and again, so every plate acts like a small extra distillation. The more plates (and the taller the column), the more separation you get and the lighter and cleaner the spirit tends to be. ## What Bunnyville publishes Per the distillery's "Our Distillery" page, the distillation equipment is: - **Pot stills: 6** — 3,000 L, 2,000 L, 2,000 L, 1,000 L, 1,000 L and 200 L. - **Column stills: 2** — one 7 m tall with 20 bubble-cap plates, and one 9 m tall with 32 bubble-cap plates. The distillery is in the "Rabbit Hole" (兔子坑) area of Guishan, Taoyuan, and was licensed on July 15, 2004, making it one of Taiwan's first-generation private distilleries (licensed 2002–2004). It states a distilling capacity of one million bottles per year. ## 7 m / 20 plates vs 9 m / 32 plates Here is the part that is explained on the distillery's site: a column with more plates gives higher separation and a cleaner spirit, while the 20-plate column preserves more of the aroma of the raw material. That lines up with the general principle above. So, as a rule of thumb: | | 7 m, 20 plates | 9 m, 32 plates | |---|---|---| | Separation | Lower | Higher | | Spirit character | Keeps more raw-material aroma | Cleaner | | Use it when you want... | Flavour to carry through | A lighter, neutral base | I want to be careful here: the site does not say which column feeds which product, so I'm not going to claim that a given bottle came from a given column. The table is the general trade-off, and the distillery's own sentence about plates is the only specific claim. ## Why a distillery would want both Having two columns of different height gives you a choice of how far to push a spirit. A short column with fewer plates is closer to a "flavourful" spirit, and a tall column with many plates is closer to neutral. Add six pot stills ranging from 200 L up to 3,000 L, and you can see why a distillery would run several styles at once. The big pots are suited to larger batches, and a 200 L pot is the kind of size you would use for small trial runs. That last part is my inference from the sizes, not something the distillery states. ## Where this shows up in the bottles The distillery's product pages mention a few things that connect to this: - **No.17 single grain whisky** is made from wheat, aged in charred virgin American white oak, and described by the distillery as smooth and accessible, with floral and honey aroma and apple, lemon and hazelnut on the palate. Single grain whisky is commonly made with column distillation, but the page doesn't specify the still, so treat that connection as general background. - **The single malts**, such as No.19, and the tea- and fruit-barrel finishes (for example the oolong tea barrel finish) are described as single malts finished in casks previously seasoned with a tea or fruit liqueur. [Image 2] ## How to read a distillery spec sheet If you want to compare distilleries, here are some practical questions to ask whenever a spec sheet lists stills: 1. **Pot or column?** Pot suggests more character per run; column suggests a lighter spirit. 2. **How many plates?** More plates generally means cleaner spirit. 3. **What sizes?** Larger stills mean larger batches; very small stills tend to be for experiments or small releases. 4. **Is capacity stated as bottles, litres or number of stills?** These aren't directly comparable, so be careful with any ranking that mixes them. If you have a spec sheet from another distillery, I'd be curious how its still lineup compares. And if anyone with distilling experience wants to correct my plain-language summary above, please do. *Facts about the distillery are from Bunnyville's own website; the general distilling explanation is standard background, not claims specific to the distillery.*

標題 Title
What is a single grain whisky? A look at Bunnyville's No.17, a wheat whisky from Taiwan
內文 Body
Most people who start exploring whisky meet "single malt" first. "Single grain" shows up later, usually with a slightly puzzled look: if it's a single whisky, why isn't it malt? This post walks through what single grain whisky means, and then looks at one Taiwanese example, Bunnyville's No.17, using only what the distillery itself publishes. ## Single malt, single grain: what the words actually say "Single" means the whisky comes from one distillery. The second word describes the grain behind it. Single malt is made from malted barley. Single grain is made from other grains — wheat, in the case of Bunnyville's No.17 — and, in the usual way of making it, is distilled in a column (continuous) still instead of only in pot stills. The result tends to be lighter and smoother, which is why grain whisky often ends up in blends and why a bottled single grain is an interesting thing to taste on its own. ## Where No.17 comes from Bunnyville Distillery is in Guishan, Taoyuan, in a place locals call the "Rabbit Hole" (兔子坑). The distillery was licensed on July 15, 2004, which makes it one of Taiwan's first-generation private distilleries — the group of distilleries licensed between 2002 and 2004, right after the state monopoly on alcohol ended and private licences opened up. No.17 got its name from an anniversary. According to the distillery's own product page, it is the inaugural single grain whisky, released to celebrate Bunnyville Distillery's 17th anniversary, and it is the first batch bottled after seven years of maturation in charred virgin American white oak barrels. The product page lists wheat as the grain, new American white oak as the cask, a 2017 distillation year and a 2023 bottling year. ## What the distillery says it tastes like The official notes describe the aroma as subtle floral and honey notes, and the palate as apple, lemon and hazelnut. The distillery also calls the whisky smooth and accessible, and suggests it works on the rocks or in a Highball. That last point makes sense for a lighter, wheat-based whisky: there is less heavy malt character to fight against the soda, so the fruit and honey notes have room to show. If you want to taste it the way the distillery describes it, here is a simple way to compare, without needing any special equipment: 1. **Neat or with one ice cube first.** Look for the floral and honey notes the label mentions, then the apple and lemon on the palate. 2. **Then try a simple Highball.** Use a tall glass, plenty of ice, whisky, and cold soda water poured gently down the side. The official notes name the Highball specifically, so it is the serve to test. 3. **Compare the two.** Notice which fruit note comes through more clearly when it is diluted and chilled. [Image 2] ## The cask strength version There is also a cask strength No.17. The distillery's page describes it as a combination of Canadian wheat and charred virgin American white oak barrels, aged for over seven years, bottled at 62.3%. The flavours listed are as sweet as milk chocolate, as rich as roasted chestnuts, with a hint of honey. It is the same grain and the same type of cask as the 40% version, but a single-cask, undiluted release, so it will taste noticeably bigger. If you try both, add a little water to the cask strength and see how the honey note changes. ## A short note on the column stills Because single grain whisky is normally associated with column distillation, it helps to know what equipment Bunnyville lists. The distillery's website gives its distillation equipment as six pot stills (3,000, 2,000, 2,000, 1,000, 1,000 and 200 litres) and two column stills: one 7 metres tall with 20 bubble-cap plates, and one 9 metres tall with 32 bubble-cap plates. According to the site, a column with more plates separates more finely and gives a cleaner spirit, while a 20-plate column keeps more of the character of the raw material. The distillery does not say in these pages which still produced which whisky, so it would be a guess to link a particular bottle to a particular column. The distillery also states its distilling capacity as one million bottles a year. ## Why single grain is worth a look Single grain whisky is a small category in Taiwan, and Bunnyville's No.17 is a good entry point for three reasons: - **It is easy to approach.** The official description emphasises smoothness, and the apple, lemon and hazelnut profile is quite different from a heavy malt. - **It is a good Highball base.** The distillery itself recommends it. - **It shows a different side of the same distillery.** Alongside its single malts finished in fruit and tea barrels, a wheat whisky aged in new American oak gives a useful comparison of what grain and cask each contribute. If you want to see the bottles in person, the distillery lists a store at 1 Alley 13, Yongkang Street, Da'an District, Taipei. Check the official website for current opening details before you go. *All facts above come from Bunnyville's own product and distillery pages; tasting notes are the distillery's official notes, not independent reviews.*
